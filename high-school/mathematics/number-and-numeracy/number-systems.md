<h2>Why number systems matter</h2><p>Mathematics uses different sets of numbers because different problems require different kinds of values. Knowing which number system a value belongs to helps you choose appropriate methods and communicate precisely.</p>
<h2>Key number sets</h2><ul><li><strong>Natural numbers:</strong> counting numbers such as 1, 2, 3, ...</li><li><strong>Whole numbers:</strong> natural numbers together with 0.</li><li><strong>Integers:</strong> positive and negative whole numbers, including 0.</li><li><strong>Rational numbers:</strong> numbers that can be written as a fraction a/b where a and b are integers and b is not 0.</li><li><strong>Irrational numbers:</strong> numbers that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers, such as √2.</li><li><strong>Real numbers:</strong> rational and irrational numbers together.</li></ul>
<h2>Worked example</h2><p>Classify −7, 0.25 and √2 as precisely as possible. −7 is an integer, rational and real number. 0.25 is rational and real because it equals 1/4. √2 is irrational and real.</p>
<h2>Common mistake</h2><p>A number can belong to more than one set. Saying that −7 is only an integer is incomplete; every integer is also rational and real.</p>
<h2>Practice</h2><ol><li>Which number set contains −3?</li><li>Is 0.125 rational or irrational?</li><li>Is √5 rational or irrational?</li></ol><details><summary>Show answers</summary><ol><li>Integers, rational numbers and real numbers.</li><li>Rational, because 0.125 = 1/8.</li><li>Irrational.</li></ol></details>
