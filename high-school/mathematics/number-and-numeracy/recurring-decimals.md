## Learning objectives
Identify recurring decimals, use algebra to convert them to fractions, and distinguish recurring decimals from terminating and irrational decimals.

## What is a recurring decimal?
A recurring decimal has a digit or block of digits that repeats indefinitely. A bar indicates the repeating block:
$$0.\overline3=0.333\ldots,$$
$$0.\overline{27}=0.272727\ldots.$$

Every recurring decimal is rational because it can be written as a ratio of integers.

## Converting a simple recurring decimal
Let $x=0.\overline3$. Then $10x=3.\overline3$. Subtract:
$$10x-x=3.\overline3-0.\overline3,$$
so $9x=3$ and $x=1/3$.

For $x=0.\overline{27}$:
$$100x=27.\overline{27},$$
so $99x=27$ and $x=27/99=3/11$.

## Mixed recurring decimals
For a decimal with a non-repeating part, multiply once to move past the non-repeating digits and again by a power of 10 matching the repeat length. Subtract the equations so the repeating tails cancel.

For example, if $x=0.1\overline6$, then $10x=1.\overline6$ and $100x=16.\overline6$. Subtracting gives $90x=15$, so $x=1/6$.

## Relationship with fractions
Some fractions terminate because their simplified denominator contains only factors 2 and/or 5. Other rational fractions produce recurring decimals. For example, $1/3$ recurs while $1/8=0.125$ terminates.

## Common mistakes
- Multiplying by the wrong power of 10.
- Forgetting to subtract equations carefully.
- Treating a recurring decimal as irrational.
